The Hormone Supplement Guide

Which supplements are worth a look for your hormones, sorted by goal and rated on the research.

Start here

Start With Lunch, Then Read This

If you have the Hormone Lunchbox, you already have the foundation. Cruciferous vegetables most days give your body the raw material to make DIM on its own, and the sulfur foods give it what it needs to make glutathione. Food does that every day, for the price of a cabbage.

Supplements come after that. Some have good human research behind them. Many are sold on a lab study in mice and a nice label.

This guide covers 13 ingredients across five goals: hormone support, cravings and blood sugar, gut, energy, and strength and healthy aging. For each one you get what it is, what the human studies found, the amounts the studies used, and who should be careful.

Every ingredient carries one of three ratings:

Strong. Several good human trials, pooled in a review, pointing the same way.

Promising. Real human trials, but small, short, or measuring a lab marker rather than how you feel.

Early. A few small human studies at most. Worth knowing about. Not worth expecting much from yet.

Early is not the same as useless. It means nobody has run the big trial yet.

The one big idea: food first, every day. A supplement fills a gap you can name. If you cannot name the gap, you probably do not need the bottle.

Goal 1 of 5

Hormone Support

The 3am wake-ups, the hot flashes, the cycle that changed in your forties. This is the goal most women come to supplements for, and it is also where the research is thinnest. Read the ratings closely here.

1

DIM (Diindolylmethane)

Promising for estrogen metabolism, Early for symptoms

What it is. the compound your body makes from cruciferous vegetables, the same one the Lunchbox is built around. As a supplement it comes ready-made.

What the research says. in a 2017 trial of 130 women on tamoxifen, DIM shifted how the body breaks down estrogen toward the 2-hydroxy pathway and raised SHBG, a protein that carries estrogen in the blood. A small 2004 pilot (19 women) found the same shift. These are lab markers. No trial has yet shown fewer hot flashes or better sleep from DIM.

Typical amount in studies. 108 to 300 mg a day.

Be careful if you take tamoxifen (DIM lowered its active forms in that trial) or use an estrogen patch or other HRT, since DIM changed estrogen levels in women on patches. Ask your prescriber first.

Sources: Thomson et al., 2017, PMID 28560655; Dalessandri et al., 2004, PMID 15623462; Newman & Smeaton, 2025, PMID 40298801.

2

Hops Extract and Xanthohumol

Promising for hot flashes, Early for xanthohumol itself

What it is. the flower used in beer. Hops carry several plant compounds, including 8-prenylnaringenin, a plant estrogen, and xanthohumol.

What the research says. in a 2015 trial of 120 women in early menopause, hop tablets cut hot flashes more than placebo over 12 weeks. A smaller European trial of 67 women, using a hop extract standardized to 8-prenylnaringenin, found less hot flashing than placebo at 6 weeks but no clear difference in overall symptoms at 12. Xanthohumol is a different compound: its main human trial (30 adults, 8 weeks) tested safety, and it was well tolerated at 24 mg a day. Its effect on hormones in people is still unstudied.

Typical amount in studies. hop extracts vary too much to compare; xanthohumol, 24 mg a day.

Be careful if you have a hormone-sensitive condition, or are pregnant or nursing.

Sources: Aghamiri et al., 2015, PMID 25982391; Heyerick et al., 2006, PMID 16321485; Langley et al., 2021, PMID 33629812.

3

Soy Isoflavones

Promising, with mixed results

What it is. plant compounds from soybeans that act a little like a weak estrogen. Genistein is the main one.

What the research says. a 2012 review pooled 17 trials and found soy isoflavones cut hot flash frequency by about 21% and severity by about 26% compared with placebo, with higher-genistein products doing better. The trials disagreed with each other a lot, and the NIH's complementary health center describes the overall results as inconsistent. Expect a modest effect, if any, and give it weeks.

Typical amount in studies. about 54 mg of isoflavones a day (the median), for 6 weeks to 12 months.

Be careful if you have a personal or strong family history of blood clots, heart disease, or breast, uterine or ovarian cancer. Guidelines say to avoid soy-based products then. Long-term safety is not established.

Sources: Taku et al., 2012, PMID 22433977; NCCIH, Menopausal Symptoms: In Depth.

Goal 2 of 5

Cravings and Blood Sugar

No supplement has good evidence for switching off cravings directly. What has evidence is blood sugar, and steadier blood sugar is where most people start.

4

Psyllium (Soluble Fiber)

Strong for blood sugar, when it is high

What it is. the husk of a plant seed. In water it forms a gel that slows how fast a meal is absorbed.

What the research says. a 2015 review of 35 randomized trials found psyllium taken before meals lowered fasting blood sugar and A1C in people with type 2 diabetes. It helped a little in people with prediabetes, and did nothing measurable in people whose blood sugar was already normal.

Typical amount in studies. taken before meals for several weeks; the dose varied by trial. Take it with a full glass of water.

Be careful if you take other pills. Fiber can slow how much medication you absorb, so take it a couple of hours apart and check timing with your pharmacist. If you take diabetes medication, ask first, since your numbers may drop. Build up slowly to avoid bloating.

Sources: Gibb et al., 2015, PMID 26561625.

5

Berberine

Strong for blood sugar in type 2 diabetes, Early for anyone else

What it is. a yellow compound from plants such as goldenseal, barberry and Chinese goldthread.

What the research says. a 2021 review pooled 46 randomized trials in people with type 2 diabetes and found berberine lowered A1C, fasting blood sugar and after-meal blood sugar, most often alongside standard diabetes care. Every trial in that review was in people with type 2 diabetes, so it says little about anyone else.

Typical amount in studies. 600 mg to 1.5 grams a day in most of the trials.

Be careful if you take any prescription. Berberine slows the liver enzymes that clear many common drugs, and with diabetes medication it can push blood sugar too low. Not for pregnancy, breastfeeding or infants: the berberine in goldenseal can harm newborns. Clear this one with your doctor before you start.

Sources: Guo et al., 2021, PMID 34956436; Bathaei et al., 2025, PMID 39141022; NCCIH, Goldenseal.

Goal 3 of 5

Gut

Your gut is where most of the fiber in the Lunchbox does its work. Two ingredients are worth knowing here.

6

Inulin (Prebiotic Fiber)

Promising

What it is. a fiber, usually from chicory root, that your gut bacteria feed on. Onions, garlic and leeks carry it too.

What the research says. a 2014 review of 5 trials (252 people with chronic constipation) found inulin improved stool frequency, consistency and transit time. It did not improve pain or bloating. A 2025 trial of 39 adults found 12 grams a day for four weeks improved regularity and raised the gut bacteria that make butyrate.

Typical amount in studies. about 12 grams a day.

Be careful if you have IBS or bloat easily. Inulin is one of the gassier fibers. Start with a teaspoon and build up over two weeks.

Sources: Collado Yurrita et al., 2014, PMID 25208775; Puhlmann et al., 2025, PMID 41233756.

7

Probiotics

Early for everyday gut health, and it depends on the strain

What it is. live bacteria or yeasts, sold as capsules, powders and in fermented foods like yogurt and kefir.

What the research says. the NIH's complementary health center says different strains do different things, and for most uses researchers still do not know which ones help. The best evidence is for preventing diarrhea while you take antibiotics. For general gut health in healthy adults, the data is thin. A strain that helped in one study tells you nothing about a different strain on another label.

Typical amount in studies. varies by strain. Look for the full strain name on the label, not just the species.

Be careful if you have a weakened immune system or a serious illness, where the risk is higher. Some products have been found to contain organisms not on the label.

Sources: NCCIH, Probiotics: What You Need To Know.

Goal 4 of 5

Energy

Low energy has a long list of causes. Two of them, low iron and low B12, are common in women, easy to test for, and fixable. Start with the blood test, then the bottle.

8

Iron (Only If Your Levels Are Low)

Strong, for women with low iron stores

What it is. the mineral your red blood cells use to carry oxygen. Heavy periods are a common reason women run low.

What the research says. in a 2012 trial, 198 women aged 18 to 53 with unexplained tiredness and low ferritin (an iron store marker below 50) but no anemia took 80 mg of iron a day or placebo for 12 weeks. Fatigue dropped by 48% on iron and 29% on placebo. That only applies when your stores are low.

Typical amount in studies. 80 mg of elemental iron a day, for 12 weeks.

Be careful if you have not had a ferritin test. The trial only included women whose stores were low, so it says nothing about iron for tiredness when your levels are fine. Too much iron can be harmful, so take it on a test result and recheck. Ask your pharmacist about timing with other medications.

Sources: Vaucher et al., 2012, PMID 22777991.

9

Vitamin B12 (Only If Your Levels Are Low)

Strong for fixing a deficiency, no evidence for extra energy if you are not low

What it is. a vitamin from animal foods that your body needs for red blood cells and nerves.

What the research says. a 2025 review for family doctors puts B12 deficiency at about 2 to 3% of US adults. The higher-risk groups are older adults, people on metformin or acid-reducing drugs like omeprazole, and people who eat little or no animal food. Tiredness and brain fog are common signs. Oral B12 works as well as shots for most people.

Typical amount in studies. set by your doctor once a deficiency is confirmed.

Be careful if you take metformin or an acid reducer long term. Ask for a B12 test. Levels that stay very high on repeated tests are also a reason to see your doctor.

Sources: Patel & McGuirk, 2025, PMID 40961307.

10

Magnesium

Early

What it is. a mineral used in more than 300 reactions in the body. Leafy greens, nuts, beans and whole grains are the best food sources.

What the research says. a 2021 review found 3 small trials (151 older adults with insomnia). On magnesium, people fell asleep about 17 minutes faster than on placebo. Total sleep time was not clearly better, and the reviewers rated the evidence low quality. Women need 310 to 320 mg a day from all sources.

Typical amount in studies. the trials used various forms and doses, under 1 gram a day.

Be careful if you have kidney problems, where extra magnesium can build up. When people get too much magnesium, it is usually from supplements or magnesium laxatives, not food. Ask your pharmacist about timing with other medications.

Sources: Mah & Pitre, 2021, PMID 33865376; MedlinePlus, Magnesium in diet.

Goal 5 of 5

Strength and Healthy Aging

Muscle is what keeps you strong and steady on your feet as you get older. The two ingredients here with the best data both work alongside strength training, and that is where the research was done.

11

Creatine

Strong, alongside strength training

What it is. a compound your muscles use for short bursts of energy. Your body makes some, and red meat and fish supply more. Women store 70 to 80% less than men.

What the research says. a 2021 review of trials in older adults found creatine plus strength training built more lean mass and strength than training alone. A 2023 review of 10 trials measuring muscle directly found the extra gain is real but small. A 2021 review focused on women found benefits for strength before menopause, and for muscle and bone after it when paired with training.

Typical amount in studies. 5 grams a day or less in many trials; some trials in women after menopause used more.

Be careful if you have kidney disease, or are pregnant or nursing. Ask your doctor first.

Sources: Forbes et al., 2021, PMID 34199420; Burke et al., 2023, PMID 37432300; Smith-Ryan et al., 2021, PMID 33800439.

12

Protein (Powder, If Food Falls Short)

Strong, alongside strength training

What it is. whey, pea, soy or other protein in powder form. Useful only if you are not getting enough from meals.

What the research says. a 2018 review of 49 trials (1,863 people) found protein supplements added strength and muscle on top of a strength training program. The extra benefit stopped at about 1.6 grams of protein per kilogram of body weight a day, from all sources. For a 150 lb woman that is about 110 grams. The benefit was smaller with age, which is one reason to spread protein through the day.

Typical amount in studies. enough to bring total daily protein up to about 1.6 grams per kilogram of body weight. More than that added nothing.

Be careful if you have kidney disease, where your doctor may set a protein limit. Check labels for added sugar.

Sources: Morton et al., 2018, PMID 28698222.

13

Glutathione (Oral, Including Liposomal)

Promising for raising your levels, Early for anything you would feel

What it is. an antioxidant your liver makes from three amino acids. The sulfur foods in the Lunchbox supply the one in short supply, cysteine.

What the research says. whether swallowed glutathione gets into your body has long been debated. A 2015 trial of 54 adults found 250 or 1,000 mg a day raised glutathione in blood and cells by up to 30 to 35% over six months, and levels fell back a month after stopping. A 2018 pilot of 12 adults found a liposomal form raised levels within two weeks; the company that makes it paid for that study. A 2011 trial of 40 adults on 1,000 mg a day for four weeks found no change at all. None of these measured how people felt.

Typical amount in studies. 250 to 1,000 mg a day.

Be careful if you are pregnant or nursing; it has not been studied there.

Sources: Richie et al., 2015, PMID 24791752; Sinha et al., 2018, PMID 28853742; Allen & Bradley, 2011, PMID 21875351.

Before you buy

What To Skip Or Be Careful With

Proprietary blends with no doses. If the label lists a "blend" with one total weight, you cannot tell whether you are getting a studied amount of anything. The amounts in this guide only mean something when the label shows them.

Hormone formulas that stack ten herbs. More ingredients usually means less of each one. Most of those stacks have never been tested as a combination.

Black cohosh, on its own. A 2012 Cochrane review of 16 trials (2,027 women) found no difference from placebo for hot flashes. A newer review is more positive for some extracts, so it stays mixed. Rare cases of liver injury have been reported. Stop and see a doctor if you notice dark urine, yellowing skin or belly pain.

High-dose anything without a test. Iron, B12 and vitamin D all have a test. Take the test first.

Mixing with your prescriptions. Talk to your doctor or pharmacist before adding a supplement if you use hormonal birth control, HRT or an estrogen patch, tamoxifen, thyroid medication, a blood thinner, or diabetes medication. DIM, soy, hops and berberine are the ones on this list most likely to matter.

"Natural" is not the same as safe. Supplements do not go through the testing medications do before they are sold, and quality varies from brand to brand.

Where to start

Where To Start

Keep the Lunchbox going. Cruciferous vegetables most days do more for the DIM and glutathione side of this than a capsule can promise, and they cost less.

If you want to add one thing, pick the goal that bothers you most, read its two or three entries again, and start there. One at a time, for at least a month, so you can tell whether it is doing anything.

This guide is for general education only and is not medical advice. It is not a substitute for your doctor. Talk to your doctor or pharmacist before starting any supplement, especially if you are pregnant or nursing, take any medication, use HRT or hormonal birth control, or have a medical condition.
