The Hormones You Already Make

You have probably heard about the new weight and blood-sugar medications by now. What most people never hear is this: the hormone those shots are built to copy is one your own body already makes, every single day, in your gut.

It is called GLP-1. And it has a couple of partners, GLP-2 and GIP, that work right alongside it. Together they are the reason a good meal makes you feel full, satisfied, and steady instead of hungry an hour later.

Here is the part that matters for your kitchen: what you put on your plate is one of the levers that decides how much of these hormones your body releases. Certain foods nudge your natural production up, gently and on your own terms. No prescription, no injection. Just food your body already knows what to do with.

This guide walks you through those foods. What they are, why they work, simple ways to use them, and a short stack of recipes that put them together. Think of it as eating in a way that works with your body instead of against it.

A quick, honest note before we start. Food is not a drug, and this guide is not medical advice. The natural rise you get from a great meal is real, but it is gentle and brief, nothing like the dose in a medication. So we are not here to promise you will "skip the shot." We are here to help you build a way of eating that supports your own hormones, your fullness, and your energy. If you are on or considering any GLP-1 medication, keep talking with your healthcare provider. Food and good habits sit alongside that conversation, they do not replace it.

The one big idea: GLP-1 and GLP-2 are made in your gut in response to food. Feed your gut the right things, in the right order, and you help it do more of what it is already built to do.

How Food Moves These Hormones

Your intestine is lined with special cells (the experts call them L-cells) that release GLP-1 and GLP-2 when they sense food. Three things on your plate flip that switch the hardest:

1. Protein. This is the most reliable trigger of all. Protein-rich foods signal fullness fast and steady your blood sugar. Dairy protein is especially well studied, partly because the calcium that rides along with it makes the effect even stronger.

2. Fiber that feeds your gut. The soft, soluble fiber in oats, barley, beans, and many vegetables is not digested up top. It travels down to your gut bacteria, which ferment it into tiny compounds called short-chain fatty acids. Those compounds are a direct "release now" signal for both GLP-1 and GLP-2. This is also the main way food helps keep your gut lining healthy, which is GLP-2's whole job.

3. The right kind of fat. Fat is a strong trigger too, but the type matters. In head-to-head studies, the good fat in olive oil beat butter for raising these hormones. So this is about leaning toward olive oil, avocado, and nuts, not piling on fat everywhere.

And two free levers that cost you nothing:

4. Eat your protein and veggies before your carbs. Same meal, same food, bigger hormone response, just from the order.

5. Slow down and actually chew. Eating slower and chewing your food (whole, not blended to mush) raises GLP-1 more. Your gut needs a moment to notice the meal.

That is the whole playbook. The rest of this guide is just showing you which foods do it best, and how to make them taste good enough to keep eating.

The Foods That Do It Through Your Gut

Feed your gut bacteria the right fibers, and they release your hormones for you.

Your Gut's Secret Weapon

Here is where it gets interesting. Some foods do not trigger your hormones directly at all. Instead they feed the bacteria in your gut, and those bacteria do the work for you.

When you eat certain fibers and starches, your gut bacteria ferment them into short-chain fatty acids (compounds with names like butyrate, acetate, and propionate). Those compounds are one of the strongest natural signals telling your gut to release GLP-1 and GLP-2. So the better you feed your gut, the more it feeds you back.

The prebiotic and resistant-starch all-stars:

  • Green (slightly under-ripe) bananas
  • Cooked and cooled potatoes
  • Cooked and cooled rice
  • Oats
  • Jerusalem artichokes
  • Onions
  • Garlic
  • Asparagus
  • Chicory root
THE COOL-DOWN TRICK (your gut's secret weapon)
This is the one nobody tells you. When you cook potatoes or rice and then cool them in the fridge, some of their starch changes into what is called resistant starch. Instead of digesting fast up top and spiking your blood sugar, it survives all the way to your gut bacteria, who turn it into those hormone-releasing compounds.
Cooled is best. Eating them cold gives you the most resistant starch and the gentlest blood-sugar response. And here is the good news: even if you gently reheat them, you keep a good chunk of the benefit. So cook a big batch, cool it overnight, and you are set.
Who actually wants to eat cold potatoes and rice, though? Nobody. That is why we built two recipes around the cooled versions that taste great: an avocado-oil potato salad and a creamy monk-fruit rice pudding. Turn to the recipes to make resistant starch delicious.
PART THREE · THE FORMULA

The GLP Plate

One simple plate you can build for any meal.

Build The GLP Plate

You do not need to memorize any of this. The whole guide boils down to one simple plate you can build for any meal:

Protein + Healthy Fat + Fiber

Hit all three and you give your body the biggest natural nudge it can get from a single meal. Protein and fiber trigger the hormones, the good fat stretches your fullness, and together they keep your blood sugar smooth.

Five plates that nail the formula:

  1. Grilled salmon + roasted vegetables + quinoa
  2. Greek yogurt + berries + walnuts
  3. Eggs + avocado + oatmeal
  4. Lentil soup + a drizzle of olive oil + a side salad
  5. Chicken breast + broccoli + sweet potato

Build your plate this way most of the time, eat your protein and veggies before your carbs, slow down and chew, and you are doing the real work. Everything else is details.

Salmon Cakes

Protein and omega-3 fats from the salmon, plus a little fiber from the binder. Batch-friendly and freezer-friendly.

16gfat
IngredientAmount

Egg1

Dijon mustard1 tbsp

Lemon, zest and juice½

Extra-virgin olive oil, for the pan1 tbsp
Method
  1. Flake the salmon into a bowl and mix in the egg, oats, mustard, green onion, lemon, salt, and pepper.
  2. Form into 8 small patties and press firm.
  3. Warm the olive oil in a pan over medium heat.
  4. Cook the cakes 3 to 4 minutes a side, until golden and set.
  5. Serve over greens with a squeeze of lemon.

Homemade Kefir

Fermented dairy brings probiotics for your gut plus protein and calcium for the direct hormone signal. Cheaper than store-bought once you have the grains.

Where to get grains: look for "live kefir grains" from a well-reviewed online culture shop, or ask a friend who already brews (they multiply, so people share).
IngredientAmount
Milk (whole or 2%)4 cups
Live kefir grains1 to 2 tbsp
Method
  1. Add the kefir grains to a clean glass jar and pour in the milk.
  2. Cover with a cloth and a rubber band (it needs to breathe, not seal).
  3. Leave on the counter, out of direct sun, 18 to 24 hours, until it smells tangy and thickens.
  4. Strain out the grains (save them for the next batch) and refrigerate the kefir.
  5. Blend with fruit to serve. Good combos: blueberries with a touch of monk fruit, or mango.

Monk-Fruit Rice Pudding

The other half of the cool-down trick. Cooked, cooled rice carries resistant starch, and here it becomes a creamy dessert sweetened with monk fruit so your blood sugar stays calm.

6gprotein
34gcarbs
7gfat
IngredientAmount
Cooked rice, cooled overnight2 cups
Milk (dairy or coconut)2 cups
Monk fruit sweetener3 to 4 tbsp
Vanilla extract1 tsp
Cinnamon½ tsp

Method

Make Your Own Ferments

Store-bought works, but making your own is cheaper and lets you skip the added sugar.

Homemade yogurt: heat 4 cups of milk to just below a boil, cool it to warm (about 110F), whisk in 2 tbsp of plain yogurt with live cultures, then keep it warm (an oven with the light on works) for 6 to 8 hours until set. Chill. Strain through cloth for thick Greek-style.

Homemade kimchi: salt 1 chopped napa cabbage for 1 to 2 hours, rinse, then toss with grated garlic, ginger, a little chili flake, green onion, and a splash of fish sauce. Pack tight into a jar, press down so the liquid covers it, and leave on the counter 2 to 4 days, tasting daily, then refrigerate.

Homemade kefir: see recipe 6.

The GLP Foods Cheat-Sheet

Stick this on the fridge.

Eat most days:

  • A protein at every meal (eggs, fish, poultry, Greek yogurt, beans)
  • A soluble fiber daily (oats, barley, beans, chia)
  • Olive oil or avocado instead of butter
  • A prebiotic or two (onion, garlic, asparagus, slightly green banana)
  • A forkful of something fermented (kimchi, kefir, sauerkraut)

The free habits:

  • Protein and veggies first, carbs last
  • Slow down, chew your food
  • Cook rice and potatoes ahead and cool them (the resistant-starch trick)

Skip the hype:

  • No single food is a shot in a bowl. Ignore any pill or powder promising "natural Ozempic." Real food, real habits, done consistently. That is the whole game.

Make It Stick

You do not have to overhaul your kitchen this week. Pick two or three things from this guide and make them automatic.

Start here:

  1. Add a real protein to every breakfast.
  2. Cook a batch of barley or beans on Sunday.
  3. Swap butter for olive oil, and eat your protein before your carbs.

Do that consistently and you are already working with your body's own hormones, no prescription required. Small plates, repeated often, beat a perfect plan you cannot keep.

Here is to eating in a way that finally feels like it is on your side.
