These 8 tips go past it, to the foods and supplements with human studies behind them for skin. The first four are foods, each also sold as a supplement. The last four are supplements. For each one you get what it is, what the studies found, the amounts they used, and who should be careful.

Every tip carries one of three ratings:

Strong. Several good human trials, pooled in a review, pointing the same way.

Promising. Real human trials, but small, short, paid for by the company that sells the ingredient, or measuring a lab marker rather than how your skin looks.

Early. A few small human studies at most. Nobody has run the big trial yet, so expect little for now.

What to Eat

Four foods with skin trials behind them, past the protein and vitamin C already in your routine. Each is also sold as a supplement.

Tip 1 · Protection

Tomatoes, Cooked With Olive Oil

Promising, with mixed results
What

Tomatoes carry lycopene, the red pigment. Cooked tomato products like paste and sauce, eaten with some fat, are the richest everyday source.

Studies

In a 2001 trial of 19 adults, 40 grams of tomato paste with olive oil every day for 10 weeks gave 40% less redness after UV light than olive oil alone. A 2011 trial of 20 women found 55 grams of paste a day for 12 weeks gave less redness and less damage in skin samples. A 2024 review of 21 trials found tomato and lycopene cut sun redness and helped skin tone. Not every study agrees: a small 2015 trial found no protection from either tomato paste or a lycopene capsule, and a 2025 review found lycopene and other carotenoids did not change how easily skin burned.

Amount

40 to 55 grams of tomato paste a day (about 16 mg of lycopene), with olive oil, for 10 to 12 weeks.

Careful

If you are counting on it in place of sunscreen. The best results in these trials were less redness, so keep the sunscreen.

Sources: Stahl et al., 2001, PMID 11340098; Rizwan et al., 2011, PMID 20854436; Zhang et al., 2024, PMID 36606553; Sokoloski et al., 2015, PMID 26024575; Yang et al., 2025, PMID 40761858.

Tip 2 · Protection

Oily Fish (Omega-3s)

Early for skin
What

Fats found mainly in cold-water fish such as salmon, mackerel and tuna, in shellfish, and in fish oil. Flaxseed, chia seeds and walnuts carry a plant form.

Studies

In a 2013 trial of 79 women, a high-dose omega-3 oil for three months seemed to reduce how much sunlight weakened the skin's immune response, but the overall result was not statistically significant. A 2013 French study of 2,919 adults found women who ate more of the fish omega-3 EPA had less severe sun aging, which shows a link but cannot prove cause. A small 2014 trial of 45 people with mild to moderate acne found 2 grams a day for 10 weeks cut spots. US dietary guidelines advise 8 ounces or more of seafood a week.

Amount

2 grams a day of EPA and DHA (the acne trial) up to 5 grams of omega-3 oil a day (the sun trial). As food, 8 ounces or more of seafood a week.

Careful

If you have a fish or shellfish allergy. Fish oil side effects are usually mild: unpleasant taste, bad breath, heartburn, nausea. If you take any prescription, ask your doctor before a high-dose supplement.

Sources: NCCIH, Omega-3 Supplements: What You Need To Know; Pilkington et al., 2013, PMID 23364005; Latreille et al., 2013, PMID 23938188; Jung et al., 2014, PMID 24553997.

Tip 3 · Protection

Cocoa and Green Tea

Promising as a group, with mixed single trials
What

Both are rich in flavanols, plant compounds with antioxidant effects. The trials used specially made high-flavanol cocoa drinks and green tea drinks or extracts.

Studies

In a 2006 trial, women who drank high-flavanol cocoa daily for 12 weeks had 25% less redness after UV light, with better skin moisture and texture. A 2014 trial of 74 women eating high-flavanol chocolate for 12 weeks found no protection. Green tea is split the same way: a 2011 trial of 60 women drinking green tea catechins for 12 weeks found 25% less sun redness and better elasticity, and a 2015 trial of 50 adults on green tea extract capsules for three months found no difference from placebo. Pooled, a 2025 review of 40 trials found flavanols and other polyphenols did help with sun aging.

Amount

About 320 mg of cocoa flavanols a day; 1,000 to 1,400 mg of green tea catechins a day; for about 12 weeks.

Careful

If you are thinking of green tea extract pills. Rare liver injury has been reported, mainly from extracts in tablet or capsule form; green tea as a drink has no reported safety concerns in adults. Green tea extract can also lower the effect of some medications, including nadolol, atorvastatin and raloxifene. Green tea contains caffeine, so keep it moderate in pregnancy.

Sources: Heinrich et al., 2006, PMID 16702322; Mogollon et al., 2014, PMID 24970388; Heinrich et al., 2011, PMID 21525260; Farrar et al., 2015, PMID 26178731; Yang et al., 2025, PMID 40761858; NCCIH, Green Tea.

Tip 4 · Cell renewal

Spermidine (Wheat Germ, Soy, Beans and Mushrooms)

Early. No skin trial in people yet
What

A compound found in every living cell, involved in how cells grow and renew. Your body makes some, and the rest comes from food, mostly plants. Wheat germ and soybeans have the most. Beans, lentils, peas, mushrooms, hazelnuts, pistachios, broccoli, cauliflower, spinach and green beans also carry a good amount. Fruit and most cheeses carry little.

Studies

Most of the work so far is in the lab and in animals, and the data in people is thin. In a 2017 trial of 100 adults, a spermidine-based tablet kept more hair in its growing phase after 90 days; two of the authors were company employees and a third was a paid consultant to the same company. No trial has tested spermidine on skin. A 2023 study gave 12 adults 15 mg a day and found blood spermidine did not go up at all (another compound, spermine, did), and the authors doubt lower doses do anything short term. In a 2018 Austrian study, people who ate the most spermidine lived longer. A study like this shows a link but cannot prove cause.

Amount

0.9 mg a day from wheat germ extract (a year-long memory trial, which found no change in memory); 15 mg a day (the blood level study); 40 mg a day was tested for safety for four weeks in older men.

Careful

If you are pregnant or nursing. It has not been studied there.

Sources: Muñoz-Esparza et al., 2019, PMID 31355206; Rinaldi et al., 2017, PMID 29214104; Senekowitsch et al., 2023, PMID 37111071; Kiechl et al., 2018, PMID 29955838; Schwarz et al., 2022, PMID 35616942; Keohane et al., 2024, PMID 39405978.

Part 2 of 2

What to Take

Four supplements with skin trials behind them. Zinc is for breakouts; the other three are for moisture and the look of fine lines. Read the amounts: a supplement only matches a study when its label shows the same dose.

Tip 5 · Breakouts

Zinc

Promising for breakouts, alongside treatment
What

A mineral used for wound healing, cell growth and your immune system. Beef, pork, lamb and the dark meat of chicken are the best sources, then nuts, whole grains and legumes. The zinc in plant foods is harder to absorb.

Studies

A 2020 review found people with acne have lower zinc in their blood, and that zinc treatment reduced red, inflamed spots, on its own or added to other treatment. In a 2001 trial of 332 people, 30 mg of zinc a day worked, but the antibiotic minocycline worked better (63% of people clearly improved on the antibiotic, 31% on zinc).

Amount

30 mg of zinc a day, for 3 months. Women need 8 mg a day from all sources, so this is well above that.

Careful

If you take it long term. High doses can cause nausea, cramps and diarrhea, and over time can leave you short of copper, iron or magnesium. Stomach side effects were the most common problem with zinc in acne trials. Use it for a set time, like the 3 months in the trial.

Sources: Yee et al., 2020, PMID 32860489; Dreno et al., 2001, PMID 11586012; MedlinePlus, Zinc in diet; Shields et al., 2023, PMID 37878272.

Tip 6 · Moisture

Ceramides (Swallowed)

Promising for dry skin
What

Fats that play a central part in your skin's outer barrier, the layer that stops water escaping. Supplements are made from wheat, rice, konjac and other plants.

Studies

A 2022 review of skin moisture supplements found 11 ceramide trials (601 people). Pooled, ceramides raised skin moisture and cut the water lost through the skin compared with placebo. A 2020 trial of adults with dry skin found 12 weeks of a ceramide food raised skin moisture with no side effects. Most of these trials are small, and the effect on anything beyond moisture is unknown.

Amount

Very small, between 0.4 and 30 mg a day of ceramides, for 6 to 20 weeks.

Careful

If you avoid gluten. Some products are made from wheat, so check the label.

Sources: Sun et al., 2022, PMID 35719159; Tsuchiya et al., 2020, PMID 33055441.

Tip 7 · What skin is built from

Collagen Peptides

Promising, with mixed results
What

Collagen broken down into small pieces (hydrolyzed) so it dissolves in a drink. It is made from fish (marine collagen), cow, pig or chicken. Bone broth, cuts on the bone and fish with the skin on are the food versions.

Studies

A 2023 review of 26 trials (1,721 people, mostly women) found collagen improved skin moisture and elasticity compared with placebo. A 2021 review of 19 trials found the same, plus fewer wrinkles, after about 90 days. A 2025 review of 23 trials found the benefit came from the trials companies paid for, and the independent trials found none.

Amount

Most trials used 2.5 to 10 grams a day, for 8 to 12 weeks. A few used 1 gram or less.

Careful

If you have a fish allergy. Marine collagen is made from fish. Check the source on the label if you avoid beef, pork or chicken for any reason.

Sources: Pu et al., 2023, PMID 37432180; de Miranda et al., 2021, PMID 33742704; Myung & Park, 2025, PMID 40324552; Proksch et al., 2014, PMID 23949208.

Tip 8 · Moisture

Hyaluronic Acid (Swallowed)

Promising for moisture and fine lines, mostly from one company's trials
What

A substance your skin makes to hold water. It is best known in serums and fillers, and is also sold as a supplement.

Studies

In a 2017 trial of 60 adults with crow's feet, 120 mg a day for 12 weeks gave fewer wrinkles than placebo. A 2021 trial of 40 adults at the same dose found fewer wrinkles, more water in the skin's surface and better elasticity. Both trials were run by employees of the company that makes the ingredient. A 2025 review of 7 trials found real gains in moisture, elasticity and wrinkle depth, but called the trials small and asked for bigger ones. A separate 2025 review found no clear benefit for sun-related aging.

Amount

120 mg a day in most trials, 200 mg in one, for 4 to 12 weeks.

Careful

If you are pregnant or nursing. It has not been studied there.

Sources: Oe et al., 2017, PMID 28761365; Hsu et al., 2021, PMID 34203487; Amin et al., 2025, PMID 40911749; Sun et al., 2022, PMID 35719159; Yang et al., 2025, PMID 40761858.

Before you buy

What to Skip or Be Careful With

Proprietary blends with no doses. If the label lists a "blend" with one total weight, you cannot tell whether you are getting a studied amount of anything. The amounts in this guide only mean something when the label shows them.

Beauty formulas that stack ten ingredients. More ingredients usually means less of each one. Most of those stacks have never been tested as a combination.

Probiotics sold for skin. A 2022 review found no moisture benefit from probiotic bacteria or fermented foods. A 2025 review found some. Until the trials agree, this is not where to spend first.

Lycopene capsules in place of tomatoes. In a 2015 trial, tomato paste raised blood lycopene more than a capsule did.

High-dose vitamin C or zinc "just in case." Over 2,000 mg of vitamin C a day is not recommended. Zinc well above what you need can leave you short of copper. Food covers most people.

Mixing with your prescriptions. Talk to your doctor or pharmacist before adding any supplement if you take a prescription. Green tea extract is the one on this list with known interactions: it can lower the effect of nadolol, atorvastatin and raloxifene. If you take diabetes medication, a change in your carbs can change your numbers.

Natural products can still cause harm. Supplements do not go through the testing medications do before they are sold, and quality varies from brand to brand.
