Start here

The recipes in Feed Your Gut feed the good bacteria already living in your gut. Fermented foods bring in more of them, and more kinds.

What's known In a Stanford study, healthy adults built up to about six servings of fermented foods a day, things like yogurt, kefir, kimchi and kombucha, and kept it up for ten weeks. The variety of bacteria in their guts went up (Wastyk et al., Cell, 2021).

You don't need six servings. You need one fermented food you'll eat most days. The next sections show you how to find a live one at the store, how to start small, and how to make two at home in a mason jar.

None of this is medical advice. If you have a medical condition or a weakened immune system, or you're pregnant, read the caution at the end before you start.

Live or not?

A fermented food is one that bacteria or yeast have worked on, eating some of its sugars and changing it: milk into yogurt, cabbage into sauerkraut. The bacteria that did the work stay in the food, alive, until something kills them. Usually that's heat.

How to tell at the store

  • Look in the fridge case. Live ferments are kept cold. A jar that sits on a shelf at room temperature has almost always been heated so it keeps.
  • Read for the words. "Live and active cultures" on yogurt and kefir. "Raw", "unpasteurized" or "naturally fermented" on sauerkraut, kimchi and pickles.
  • Watch for "heat treated after culturing" on yogurt. It means the cultures were killed after the yogurt was made.
  • Check the ingredients for vinegar. Fermented pickles and sauerkraut are usually made with salt and water. Vinegar in the list usually means it was pickled in vinegar and never fermented.
Shelf-stable sauerkraut, in a can or a jar on the shelf, is usually heat-treated, so the bacteria are gone. It still tastes like sauerkraut. For live bacteria, buy the one in the fridge.

The store-bought list

Plain yogurtStart here

Every yogurt starts with live cultures. Look for "live and active cultures" on the tub and pass on "heat treated after culturing". Plain has no added sugar, so sweeten it yourself with fruit, like the kiwi bowl in your recipes.

KefirStart here

A tangy fermented milk you drink. It usually carries more kinds of bacteria and yeasts than yogurt. Plain kefir goes into the green smoothie in place of the yogurt and milk.

Sauerkraut from the fridgeStart here

Cabbage and salt, fermented. Buy it from the fridge case and look for "raw" or "unpasteurized". A forkful on eggs, in a sandwich, or on the cabbage and sausage stir fry.

KimchiWorth a try

Korean fermented cabbage with garlic, ginger and chili, spicy and sour. Most of it is sold in the fridge case. A spoonful on rice, eggs or the black bean bowls.

MisoWorth a try

A salty, savory paste of fermented soybeans. Many in the fridge case are unpasteurized, so read the label. Stir it in at the end, off the heat.

Brined picklesWorth a try

The cloudy ones in the fridge case, made with salt and water. Look for "fermented" on the label and no vinegar in the ingredients.

KombuchaFine, but read the label

Fermented sweet tea, fizzy and sour. It has live cultures, and the sugar varies a lot from one bottle to the next, so read for it. Some bottles carry a little alcohol too.

Aged cheeseFine, but read the label

Cheese is made with bacteria, and some aged cheeses, like Gouda and cheddar, keep live ones. Labels rarely say, so count it as a bonus and lean on the foods above.

How to like them

  • Sauerkraut: a forkful on scrambled eggs, on a turkey sandwich, or on the stir fry at the table.
  • Kefir: in the green smoothie, in place of the yogurt and milk. The banana and blueberries cover the tang.
  • Kimchi: chopped into fried rice once it's off the heat, or on top of the black bean bowls.
  • Miso: whisk a teaspoon into olive oil, lemon juice and a little honey for a salad dressing. In soup, stir it in after the pot comes off the heat, since boiling kills the cultures.
  • Yogurt: a spoonful on the chili or the lentil soup once it's in the bowl.
Heat kills the bacteria, so add fermented foods at the table, after the cooking's done.

Two jars at home, no crock

You need a quart mason jar, salt, and a kitchen scale if you have one. Salt goes by weight because a teaspoon of one salt can weigh twice as much as a teaspoon of another. Use pickling salt or fine sea salt.

Jar 1: sauerkraut

  • 1 small green cabbage (about 2 lb)
  • 2 grams of salt for every 100 grams of cabbage (about 18 grams, or 1 tablespoon of fine sea salt, for 2 lb)
  1. Pull off two outer leaves and set them aside. Quarter the cabbage, cut out the core, slice it thin and weigh it.
  2. Sprinkle on the salt in a big bowl and squeeze the cabbage with your hands for 5 to 10 minutes, until it's limp and sitting in its own juice.
  3. Pack it into the jar a handful at a time, pressing hard, until the juice rises over the top. Leave 2 inches of space.
  4. Fold a saved leaf over it and hold everything under the juice with a small jar or a zip bag of water.
  5. Put the lid on loosely, set the jar on a plate out of the sun, and press it down every day or two. Taste it from day 5. When it's sour enough for you, usually 1 to 3 weeks, tighten the lid and move it to the fridge.

Jar 2: carrot sticks in brine

  • 1 lb carrots, cut into sticks that fit the jar
  • 2 garlic cloves and a few sprigs of dill, if you like
  • 4 cups of water with 20 grams of salt stirred in (about 3½ teaspoons of fine sea salt)
  1. Stir the salt into the water until it's gone. Use filtered water if your tap water smells of chlorine.
  2. Stand the carrots up tightly in the jar with the garlic and dill, and pour in brine to cover them by an inch. Weigh them down.
  3. Loose lid, out of the sun, for 3 to 7 days. Taste from day 3, and move it to the fridge when it's tangy enough.

What's normal, and what isn't

Normal: bubbles, cloudy brine, a sour smell like pickles, and a flat white film on top. The film is a harmless yeast: skim it off.

Throw it out: fuzzy mould of any color (white, blue, green, black or pink), a rotten smell, or a slimy feel.

Fermented food myths

"Sourdough bread is full of good bacteria."

The starter is alive. Baking kills the bacteria and yeast in it, so the bread has none left by the time you eat it.

"Pickles are fermented."

Most jarred pickles are made with vinegar, which pickles the cucumbers without fermenting them. Fermented pickles are the cloudy ones in the fridge case, made with salt and water.

"Beer and wine count."

Both are fermented, and most are filtered or pasteurized before they're bottled, so there's little or nothing live in the glass. The alcohol works against your gut too.

"More is better."

The people in the Stanford study built up to six servings over weeks. Going from none to a lot at once mostly gets you gas and bloating. A forkful most days, kept up for months, is the goal.

Before you start

Start small, one food at a time. If you have a weakened immune system, are pregnant or nursing, or follow a low-histamine or low-salt diet, ask your doctor before you add fermented foods. Sauerkraut, kimchi, miso and brined pickles are salty, and fermented foods are high in histamine. Ask your doctor before you take any supplement if you're pregnant or nursing, have a medical condition, or take any medication.
