How It Gets In

Four ordinary routes repeat all day, every day, and they explain nearly every swap on this list.

Heat. Plastic is a solid holding smaller molecules loosely inside it, and warming it loosens that grip, so more of what is in the plastic moves into whatever it is touching. Oily and fatty food pulls across more than water does. That one idea is the microwave, the bottom rack of the dishwasher, the bottle left in a hot car and the scratched pan on a high flame.

Skin, and how long it stays on. What matters is contact time and surface area rather than how strong something smells. A detergent stays in the fabric against you all day, and a body wash covers all of you at once. That's why this list goes after the things you use every day without thinking, and leaves the perfume you wear once a month alone.

House dust. Furniture foam, electronics, vinyl flooring and carpet shed slowly for years, and what they shed collects in dust. It settles, it gets walked through and it goes back up into the air. Damp cloths, shoes at the door and a sealed vacuum are three versions of the same fix.

Water, drunk and breathed. Some of what is in tap water is regulated and reported. The part people miss is that a hot shower turns some of it into vapor in a small closed room, so water is something you breathe as well as drink. That's why the list has two filters.

Straight with you

This is general information about everyday products and habits at home. It is not medical advice and none of it is a diagnosis. The research here is still developing, and honest people disagree about how much any single exposure matters. What isn't really in dispute is that daily beats occasional, and that most of what is on these pages costs little or nothing.
