Every ingredient in a hydration sachet, what it does, and where else to get it

What you sweat out

Sweat is mostly water, and the main thing in it after water is sodium. How much varies a lot from person to person: some people lose a few hundred milligrams in a litre of sweat, salty sweaters lose well over a thousand. If your gym top dries with white marks on it, you're probably one of the salty ones.

You lose some potassium too, much less than sodium, and only a trace of magnesium.

Plain water puts the water back and none of the salt. After a hard class, a glass of water can leave you wanting something more. That's the gap an electrolyte drink fills.

You don't need to measure your sweat. Have a drink with some sodium in it after a session that left you soaked, and plain water the rest of the day.

The three that matter

Sodium. Helps your body hold on to the water you drink and keeps your fluid balance where it should be. You lose more of it than any other mineral.

In your drinks: the Salted Lime Lassi has 405 mg, the Banana Coconut Recovery Smoothie 280 mg, and every salted rim adds up to 290 mg more.

On its own: it's salt. A dash in your drink or a salted rim is all it takes, and sea salt, pink salt or table salt all work.

Potassium. Works with sodium to keep fluid balanced, and helps your muscles work the way they should. Most of us eat less of it than we need, workout or not.

In your drinks: this is where the smoothies shine. The Banana Coconut Recovery Smoothie has 1,065 mg, the Orange Creamsicle 970 mg, the Kiwi Coconut Cooler 840 mg.

On its own: food is the easy way. Potassium pills sold over the counter in the US carry 99 mg each at most, so a banana beats a handful of them. Ask your doctor before taking it as a supplement if you take blood pressure medication or have kidney trouble.

Magnesium. Plays a part in how your muscles work and recover. You lose only a little in sweat, but plenty of people don't get enough from food to begin with.

In your drinks: the Chocolate PB Banana Shake has 110 mg, the Blueberry Almond Smoothie 80 mg. Cocoa, nut butters and leafy greens are the big sources.

On its own: look for magnesium citrate or glycinate, which your body takes up well. Keep a supplement to 350 mg a day or less unless your doctor says otherwise; past that it tends to send you to the bathroom.

Vitamin C

Not an electrolyte, but you'll find it in a lot of hydration drinks. It's an antioxidant, and your body uses it every day.

Most people get plenty from fruit. One medium orange has about 70 mg, and the Orange Creamsicle Smoothie and the Orange Pomegranate Sunrise both use a whole one.

On its own: any plain vitamin C works. More isn't better: your body passes what it doesn't use.

The extras

Some sachets add ingredients you won't find in a kitchen. Here's what the common ones are, and what the maker of the sachet on the last page says each one is for.

Glycine. An amino acid. "Acts as a cellular osmolyte, helps support water retention within cells."

Taurine. Another amino acid, the one you've seen on energy drink cans. "Supports cellular hydration, helps support electrolyte transport."

Coconut water powder. Dried coconut water, the same thing in your drinks without the carton. "Provides naturally occurring hydration-supporting minerals."

Trace minerals. Small amounts of many minerals, often from seawater. "Supports mineral balance and cellular function."

Shilajit. A mineral-rich resin from mountain rock, used in Ayurvedic tradition for centuries. "Supports nutrient transport and mineral replenishment."

Sodium, potassium and magnesium are the ones you can count and the ones the drinks in your guide are built on. The extras are there for the reasons above. If you wanted all five on their own, that's five more tubs on your counter, which is most of the case for a sachet.
