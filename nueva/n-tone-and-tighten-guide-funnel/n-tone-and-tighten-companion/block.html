Which One Is Right For Me?

Including the parts that don't work, and the four questions that tell you which is which.

Start here

You asked for this, so here is the short version before you read seven pages.

Most of what's sold for weight management doesn't do what the front of the bottle implies. Some of it does. A few things are worth your money, several are worth a try if they're cheap, and a lot of it is expensive urine.

This guide grades everything one of four ways, and it uses them strictly.

Worth it. Real evidence, in people, at a dose you can actually buy.

Worth a try. Plausible, with small or mixed studies behind it, and cheap enough that a month of it is a reasonable thing to do.

Thin. Popular, widely sold, and the evidence doesn't support the claim on the label.

Skip. Sold hard for exactly this, and it doesn't do it.

Roughly half of what follows is Thin or Skip. If a guide likes everything, it isn't grading, it's shopping.

One more thing before we start. Nothing in here is a substitute for the training you're already doing. It sits on top of it, or it does nothing at all.

Before you spend anything

Two things beat every product in this guide, and you already have both.

The training. You're doing it. Muscle is the thing that decides what your body does with the food you eat, and you're building it. Every product below is a small nudge next to that, and any one of them taken instead of training is a waste of money.

Protein at breakfast. Thirty grams, early. It measurably reduces snacking later in the day, which is the part of the day most plans fall apart. It's in the recipes you already have.

Neither of those is a product, and that's why they go first. If you're doing both and you still want to add something, the rest of this is the answer to which something.

How to read a label in 60 seconds

Four questions. They work on any bottle, including ours, and they're most of what this guide is actually for.

1. Is the active named, or is it only in the ad? Marketing talks about an ingredient. The label has to list it. If the thing they're selling you doesn't appear on the back of the bottle with an amount beside it, it isn't in there in any amount worth having.

2. Is it at the dose the study used? This is where most claims quietly die. Plenty of products carry the right ingredient at a fraction of the amount anybody tested. Where this guide knows the studied dose, it's printed, so you can hold a label against it.

3. Was it studied in people, for the thing being claimed? Cells in a dish and mice at doses no human could swallow are not evidence about you. And an ingredient studied for blood sugar is not evidence about your waist, even though it will be sold that way.

4. Will you still be doing it in week 12? A product you have to remember three times a day with food is a product you'll stop. The best formula you won't keep taking loses to the adequate one you will.

Two smaller tells. A third party seal (NSF, Informed Choice, USP) doesn't prove a product works, it proves the bottle contains what the label says. And "clinically studied ingredient" is not "clinically studied product." The first means someone once ran a trial on a substance that appears somewhere in the bottle. The second means they tested this.

The ones with something behind them

Protein and fiber. Worth it
Not a supplement and the most effective thing on this list, which is why it's first. A protein powder counts if it's easier than cooking.

Morosil (blood orange extract). Worth it, with the funding caveat
A standardized extract of Moro blood orange, studied at 400 mg a day. In a six month trial in overweight adults, the supplemented group lost more waist (3.9 cm vs 1.7 cm) and more body mass (4.2% vs 2.2%) than placebo. That's a real human trial at a dose you can buy, which is rarer in this category than it should be. The caveat: the research base is small, and studies on a branded ingredient are usually funded by the people who sell it. Worth knowing before you decide the case is closed. Check the number on the label. 400 mg is the amount that was tested, and less than that is a different product wearing the same name.

Glucomannan and soluble fiber. Worth a try
Fiber that absorbs water and takes up room, so you eat a little less without deciding to. Around 1 g before meals, three times a day, with a full glass of water. Don't take it dry or right before lying down. It swells, and the choking risk is real.

Psyllium husk. Worth a try, and it is the cheap version of the entry above
Same mechanism as glucomannan, a fraction of the price, and it is the one most people tolerate better. A teaspoon in a large glass of water before a meal, and the water is not optional. If you already own a tub for other reasons, you do not need to buy a second fiber.

Caffeine. Worth it, within limits
It raises energy expenditure slightly and blunts appetite for a while, and it makes a hard training session feel easier, which may be the bigger effect. Two honest limits: you build tolerance to the appetite part within weeks, and it's cumulative across everything you drink and take. Count your total for the day, and keep it out of the late afternoon if you'd like to sleep, because sleep is doing more for your appetite than the caffeine is.

Saffron extract. Worth a try
Small trials suggest less snacking between meals, usually around 88 to 176 mg a day of a standardized extract. The studies are small and several were funded by the people selling the extract.

Green tea extract (EGCG). Worth a try, with a real caution
A small effect on metabolic rate, much smaller than the marketing suggests, and usually studied alongside caffeine. ⚠️ Concentrated green tea extract has been linked to liver injury, with the concern focused on high doses taken on an empty stomach. Drinking green tea isn't the issue. If you take an extract, take it with food and stop if you feel unwell.

Berberine. Worth a try, and read the cautions twice
The glucose evidence here is decent, which is rare in this guide, typically 500 mg two or three times a day. But it upsets a lot of stomachs, it interferes with the enzymes that clear many prescription drugs, and it isn't for anyone pregnant or breastfeeding. If you take any prescription medication, this is a conversation with a pharmacist, not an impulse buy.

The ones that don't hold up

CLA (conjugated linoleic acid). Thin
One of the most-sold weight ingredients there is, and it has been tested plenty. The better-run human trials find changes in body fat that are small enough to be hard to notice, and the results disagree with each other. It isn't dangerous and it isn't nothing. It just isn't what it's sold as.

Chromium. Thin
Sold everywhere for carb cravings. The trials are inconsistent, the effects are small where they exist at all, and the clearest signal is in people who were low in chromium to begin with, which is uncommon. It's safe and cheap, which is the only argument for it.

Cinnamon. Thin
Some studies show a small effect on fasting glucose and plenty show nothing. If you use it anyway, Ceylon cinnamon has very little coumarin, which the ordinary supermarket kind has a lot of, and coumarin is hard on the liver in quantity.

Apple cider vinegar. Split verdict
The liquid is a fair worth a try. A tablespoon in water before a carb-heavy meal blunts the rise afterwards a little, and the acid is what does it. The gummies are a skip. The acid is the active part, and it's the part a gummy has least of, along with the sugar it needs to taste like a sweet.

L-carnitine. Thin
The theory is that it shuttles fat into the cell to be burned, which is true and is not the bottleneck. In people who are not deficient, and almost nobody is, the trials find very little. It is safe, it is sold everywhere, and it is mostly sold on how good the mechanism sounds.

MCT oil. Thin
A real fat with a real metabolic quirk, sold as though the quirk changes the arithmetic. It is roughly 115 calories a tablespoon and those calories count like every other. Useful if it replaces something, pointless if it is added on top, and famous for sending people to the bathroom at the doses the ads suggest.

Carb blockers (white kidney bean extract). Thin
They do block some starch digestion, and the amount is smaller than the label implies. The better trials find modest effects at best, and the part that reaches your colon undigested is the part that makes you gassy.

5-HTP. Skip, on the caution alone
Sold for appetite and mood, and the appetite evidence is thin. ⚠️ It should not be combined with SSRIs, SNRIs, triptans or other serotonergic medication. That is not a small print caution, it is the entire reason this one is a skip rather than a shrug.

Exogenous ketones and keto pills. Skip
Drinking ketones raises the ketones in your blood, which is what the label promises and not what anyone wanted. It does not put you in dietary ketosis, it does not replicate the diet, and it is among the most expensive powders on the shelf.

Bitter orange and synephrine. Skip
This is what a lot of "ephedra free" formulas moved to after ephedra was pulled. The stimulant effect is real, so is the rise in blood pressure and heart rate, especially stacked with caffeine, which it usually is.

Garcinia cambogia. Skip
Heavily marketed for appetite, repeatedly tested, and the better trials find nothing beyond placebo.

Green coffee bean extract. Skip
The study that launched it was retracted and the company behind the marketing was fined over the claims. Nothing since has replaced it.

Raspberry ketones. Skip
The work behind the claim is in cells and rodents at doses no person could take. There are no useful human trials.

Detox teas and cleanses. Skip
Most work by being a laxative, usually senna. You lose water, you gain it back, and you can end up dependent on a laxative.

A note on collagen, because it's in a lot of these bottles. Collagen isn't a weight ingredient and a good product isn't pretending it is. It's there for skin, hair and joints, and that's where its evidence is. Judge it on that and it's a fair addition. Judge it as the reason a product works on your waist and you'll be disappointed, because that isn't its job.

How to tell whether it did anything

Nearly everyone buys a supplement and then changes three other things in the same week, which makes the next three months unreadable. Here is the version that gives you an answer.

Change one thing at a time, and give it four weeks. Not two. Most of what is worth taking works slowly enough that two weeks is noise, and the Morosil trial quoted earlier ran for six months.

Write down two numbers before you start. Your waist, measured at the navel, first thing in the morning, and one lift you can count, whatever the guide has you doing. Those two move for different reasons and between them they cover both halves of the job.

Do not change the training, the protein or the sleep in the same month. They are stronger than anything in this guide, so if they move, you have learned nothing about the product.

Decide at the end, on the numbers. Waist down and strength holding or climbing is a good month. Nothing moved at all, and you have your answer, and it cost you one month rather than a standing order you forgot to cancel.

One honest caveat about the first two weeks. New anything makes people eat and move a little differently without noticing, so early enthusiasm is not evidence. Week four is.

Three questions to ask whoever is selling it

Including me. Anyone who cannot answer these three without getting vague is not someone you should be buying from.

1. What is the amount, and how does it compare to the study? Not the ingredient, the number. If the answer is that it is a proprietary blend, that is a real answer and you can weigh it. If the answer is a change of subject, that is also an answer.

2. What should I notice, and when? A real answer sounds like "appetite in a couple of weeks, the waist over a couple of months, and if neither has moved by then, stop." A bad answer is a promise with no timeline attached to it, because a promise with no timeline can never be wrong.

3. What would make you tell me to stop taking it? The best people in this industry have a ready answer. It usually involves something not moving by a certain date, or a medication you are on, or the fact that a month of it costs more than you want to spend for as long as it would take to work.
